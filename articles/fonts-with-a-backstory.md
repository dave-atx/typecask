Most typefaces are inspired by other typefaces. These came from somewhere else: a highway, a license plate,
a Buenos Aires street, a cockpit, a typewriter, and the city where European printing began. Knowing where a
font came from is a good way to choose one, since you get a little bit of place along with the letters.

## The interstate

The lettering on American highway signs is officially the Federal Highway Administration's "Standard
Alphabets for Traffic Control Devices," better known as
[Highway Gothic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Highway_Gothic). ("Gothic" means sans serif here; see
Franklin Gothic in [Inspired by the classics](article:inspired-by-the-classics).) It comes in several widths,
so a long town name still fits on the sign.

@font-overpass: EXIT 42 – Barstow, next services 23 mi

**Overpass**, by [Delve Fonts](https://delvefonts.com/) and sponsored by [Red Hat](foundry:red-hat), adapts Highway Gothic for
screens, adjusting the letters so they read well at small sizes and in lighter weights. It works for
everything from body text to posters, and there's an [Overpass Mono](font:font-overpass-mono) for code.

## California

@font-barlow: Next train to San Francisco: 4 min

**Barlow**, by San Francisco designer [Jeremy Tribby](designer:jeremy-tribby), borrows from California's public
lettering: its license plates, highway signs, buses, and trains. It's slightly rounded and low in
[contrast](term:contrast), and comes in three widths (Barlow, Semi Condensed, and Condensed) with nine weights
each.

## Buenos Aires

@font-montserrat: Café Tortoni · Avenida de Mayo 825

**Montserrat** started with the old posters and storefront signs of Montserrat, one of the oldest
neighborhoods in Buenos Aires. [Julieta Ulanovsky](designer:julieta-ulanovsky) drew it to save the look of the neighborhood's early
20th-century lettering before redevelopment erased it, and released it under a free license so anyone could
use it. It's now one of the most popular fonts on the web.
[Montserrat Alternates](font:font-montserrat-alternates), also in the catalog, keeps more of the quirky
letters from the original signs.

## Signs that go up

On a crowded city street, from a corner liquor store to a grand old theater, a sign often has nowhere to go
but up, so the letters get stacked one on top of the other.

@font-bungee: OPEN 24 HOURS · ROOMS

**Bungee**, by [David Jonathan Ross](designer:david-jonathan-ross), celebrates those signs. It sets text
horizontally or vertically, and its round and diagonal letters are straight-sided so a vertical word has
clean edges. The catalog has the whole layered family: [Inline](font:font-bungee-inline),
[Outline](font:font-bungee-outline), [Shade](font:font-bungee-shade), [Hairline](font:font-bungee-hairline),
and the [color fonts](term:color-font) [Spice](font:font-bungee-spice) and [Tint](font:font-bungee-tint).

## The cockpit

@font-b612: FL350 · HDG 270 · M.78 · ETA 14:05Z

**B612** came out of a research project Airbus started in 2010 with ENAC, France's civil aviation
university, and the University of Toulouse, to find the most legible font for cockpit screens.
[Intactile Design](https://b612-font.com/) drew it, pushing the shapes of look-alike characters as far apart
as they'd go so they can't be confused at a glance. It's named after asteroid B-612, home of
[the Little Prince](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Little_Prince) in the book by the aviator Antoine de
Saint-Exupéry. [B612 Mono](font:font-b612-mono) is in the catalog too.

## The typewriter

@font-special-elite: To whom it may concern: the cat ate the carbon copy.

**Special Elite**, from [Astigmatic](foundry:astigmatic), copies two real typewriters, the Smith
Corona Special Elite Type No. NR6 and the Remington Noiseless, smudges and all. For a clean typewriter look,
use [Courier Prime](font:font-courier-prime) from
[Inspired by the classics](article:inspired-by-the-classics); use this one when the page should look like
it's been in a filing cabinet since 1962.

## Mainz

[Johannes Gutenberg](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Johannes_Gutenberg) cast his type in
[blackletter](term:blackletter), the dense, angular style of medieval manuscripts, when he started printing
in Mainz in the 1450s. Germany kept a blackletter style called
[Fraktur](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fraktur) for everyday printing well into the 20th century, and you
still see it on newspaper mastheads and beer labels.

@font-unifrakturmaguntia: Ye Olde Coffee Shoppe · Est. 1455

**UnifrakturMaguntia** is named after Mainz: Maguntia is an old Latin name for the city. It's based on Peter
Wiegel's Berthold Mainzer Fraktur, itself drawn from a 1901 typeface by Carl Albert Fahrenwaldt, and it
follows blackletter's rules properly: [ligatures](term:ligature) like ch, ck, and tz stay joined even when
the letters are spaced out.

As for the "Ye" in "Ye Olde": it was always pronounced "the." Old English wrote "th" with a letter called
[thorn](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thorn_%28letter%29), þ, and early English printers, using type from the
Continent that had no thorn, set a y in its place.
