Before screens had millions of pixels, every letter was drawn on a grid small enough to count. These fonts
bring that look back, from arcade cabinets and home computers to the terminals wired to IBM mainframes. Use
them for headings, game nights, invitations, and anything that should look like it's loading from a floppy
disk.

## The arcade

@font-press-start-2p: GAME OVER · CONTINUE? 9

**Press Start 2P**, by Cody "CodeMan38" Boisclair, recreates the screen font of Namco's 1980s arcade games.
The capitals and numbers go back to Atari's Sprint in 1977; the rest comes from Return of Ishtar (1986), one
of the first games to use lowercase letters regularly. It's drawn on an 8-pixel grid, so it's crispest at
sizes that are multiples of 8.

## The home computer

The [Commodore 64](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Commodore_64), from 1982, is still the best-selling single
computer model of all time. Commodore's next big machine, the [Amiga](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Amiga),
had a desktop called Workbench.

@font-sixtyfour: 10 PRINT "HELLO": GOTO 10

@font-workbench: Please insert volume Workbench

**[Sixtyfour](font:font-sixtyfour)** and **[Workbench](font:font-workbench)**, by
[Jens Kutilek](https://www.kutilek.de/), redraw those two machines' fonts. They have two fun extras: a
Scanlines [axis](term:axis) that opens gaps between rows of pixels like a CRT, and a Bleed axis that smears
the pixels sideways the way a monitor's slow phosphor did. You can play with both in Try It Out, right in
Typecask, or in any other app that lets you adjust a [variable font](term:variable-font). Like the
originals, they cover a limited set of characters.

## The mainframe

[IBM's 3270 terminals](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/IBM_3270), from 1971, were how office workers talked to
mainframes for decades, and plenty of banks and airlines still run software that expects one.

@font-3270: Ready; T=0.01/0.01 09:41:00

**[3270](font:font-3270)**, by [Ricardo Bánffy](https://github.com/rbanffy/3270font), has a long family
tree: it comes from the font in the x3270 emulator, which was translated from Georgia Tech's 3270tool, which
was hand-copied from a real 3270 terminal. He made it, he says, because "terminals deserve to be pretty."

@font-ibm-plex-mono: JOB 0042 ENDED · RC=0000

**IBM Plex Mono** is IBM's own modern take: the [monospaced](term:monospaced) member of the Plex family from
[Workhorses](article:workhorses), for when you want the heritage without the pixels.

## The block game

@font-monocraft: if (creeper != friend) { run(); }

**Monocraft** is a fan-made coding font based on the lettering in Minecraft, with each letter redrawn for a
monospaced grid. It has programming [ligatures](term:ligature) (the ≠ in the specimen was typed as !=), and
it even includes the
[Standard Galactic Alphabet](https://minecraft.wiki/w/Enchanting_Table#Standard_Galactic_Alphabet) from
Minecraft's enchanting table, which Minecraft borrowed from Commander Keen. It isn't affiliated with
Minecraft or Mojang.

## The near future

@font-departure-mono: Downloading 1 of 1,440 · 56 kbps

**Departure Mono**, by [Tobias Fried](https://departuremono.com/), is a [pixel font](term:pixel-font) for a
past that never quite happened. It's inspired by early command lines and graphical interfaces, the tiny
pixel fonts of the late 90s and early 2000s, and the computers in science fiction films. It's drawn on an
11-pixel grid, so set it at multiples of 11 for the sharpest result.

## Retro futures

In the 1960s, wide, squared-off typefaces like [Eurostile](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eurostile) and
Microgramma looked like the future, so science fiction borrowed them. Now they look like the 1960s' idea of
the future.

@font-space-mono: T-minus 10 and counting

**Space Mono**, from [Colophon Foundry](https://www.colophon-foundry.org/) for Google Design, brings those
1960s headline faces to a monospaced family meant for editorial headlines. It has old-style figures, arrows,
and a pile of alternates.

@font-orbitron: SHUTTLE DEPARTS GATE 9 AT 2401 HOURS

**Orbitron**, by Matt McInerney of [The League of Moveable Type](https://www.theleagueofmoveabletype.com/),
was made so future designers have an alternative to Eurostile and Bank Gothic. Its own description explains
the need: in every sci-fi movie, "all other fonts have been lost or destroyed in the apocalypse that led
humans to flee earth." It's for spaceship hulls, space station signs, and the posters for those movies.
