Every film has to put its name somewhere: on a title card, on a poster, across a spaceship’s hull. Type
designers notice. Some of the fonts below began as a single movie title someone couldn’t stop looking at;
others revive the lettering of a whole country’s film posters. And one of them was famous enough to get its own
sketch on Saturday Night Live.

## The font that got its own sketch

@apple:Papyrus: Welcome to Pandora

**Papyrus** is already on your iPhone: it’s one of the fonts built into iOS, and the only one in this article
you don’t need to install. Chris Costello drew it by hand in 1982, imagining what English might have looked like
written on papyrus two thousand years ago, and Letraset released it the next year. For decades it turned up on
tea shops, yoga studios, and church flyers.

Then James Cameron set the logo of *Avatar*, the biggest film of 2009, in a lightly modified Papyrus, and in
2017 Saturday Night Live gave Ryan Gosling a sketch about a man who can’t let it go. When *Avatar: The Way of
Water* came out, Gosling came back for *Papyrus 2*.

To see why it stands out, look at the wobbly edges, as if inked on fibrous paper, and the high, sweeping
crossbars. It’s a display face that reads as “ancient” from across a room, which is exactly what made it
everyone’s first choice, and then nobody’s.

## Title cards

Before opening credits ran over the first scene, a film opened on a card: the title, hand-lettered and
photographed, held on screen long enough to read twice. Lettering artists drew a new one for every picture, and
a few have become fonts.

@font-englebert: The Blue Angel

**Englebert** takes its cue from the title of *Der blaue Engel* (1930), the film that made Marlene Dietrich a
star. It’s casual and a little bouncy, a sans serif that still catches the eye from across a room. It’s one of
several fonts in this article by [Astigmatic](foundry:astigmatic), Brian J. Bonislawsky’s foundry, which has
made a habit of finding type in old movies.

@font-grand-hotel: Café Metropole

**Grand Hotel** comes from the title screen of *Café Metropole* (1937), starring Tyrone Power. It’s an upright
script with its letters joined, condensed so a long title fits across the screen, with a weight that suits a
bakery’s window as well as a marquee.

@font-yesteryear: The Palm Beach Story

**Yesteryear** is loosely based on the title of Preston Sturges’s *The Palm Beach Story* (1942). It’s drawn as
if with a flat-nibbed pen, a little sharper than the original, and it looks like the chrome script on the fender
of a car from the same years.

@font-miniver: Mrs. Miniver

**Miniver** started in a library, where its designer spotted the DVD of *Mrs. Miniver* (1942) and thought its
hand-lettered title looked surprisingly modern. The letters are cut through with a line inside each stroke, like
paper cutouts.

@font-shojumaru: SAYONARA

**Shojumaru** draws on the poster for *Sayonara* (1957), starring Marlon Brando. Its letters are built from
brush-like wedges, a style Hollywood once used for anything set in East Asia; Shojumaru mixes it with more
traditional letterforms, which keeps it from being a caricature.

## Posters from around the world

Hollywood wasn’t the only place painting titles by hand. Every film industry had its poster artists, and their
lettering became part of how a country’s movies looked.

@font-hahmlet: 햄릿
= Hamlet

**Hahmlet** began with a 1940s poster for a Korean *Hamlet*, lettered by an unknown artist. Minjoo Ham turned its
sharp, quirky Hangul into a full typeface, and Mark Frömberg drew Latin letters to match, so it sets Korean and
English side by side. It’s by [Hypertype](foundry:hypertype).

@font-lalezar: لاله زار
= Lalezar

**Lalezar**, by [Borna Izadpanah](designer:borna-izadpanah), revives the lettering of Iran’s Film-Farsi posters,
the popular romances, musicals, and action pictures of the 1960s and 1970s. Their titles were bold, bouncy, and
impossible to miss, and Lalezar turns them into a modern Arabic-script display face with Latin letters to match.
It’s named after Lalezar Street, Tehran’s old cinema district.

@font-rakkas: رقّاص
= Dancer

**Rakkas**, by [Zeynep Akay](designer:zeynep-akay), draws its Arabic from the Ruq’ah lettering on Egyptian movie
posters of the 1950s and 1960s, the golden age of Cairo’s studios. Its letters take different shapes depending
on their neighbors and cascade down the line like calligraphy. The Latin is an informal take on blackletter.

@font-ojuju: MASQUERADE

**Ojuju** is mostly inspired by African masquerade costumes, but its designer, Chisaokwu Joboson, also looked at
the lettering on African movie posters of the 1970s. It’s a variable font with its contrast reversed, heavier
across than up and down, and its openings change shape as it gets bolder.

@font-rowdies: NO MERCY

**Rowdies**, by [Jaikishan Patel](designer:jaikishan-patel), is inspired by Indian action cinema: rough, a little
odd in every letter, and made for posters that promise a fight. Patel drew it for action, thrillers, noir, and
crime.

## The future, as the movies see it

Science fiction needs letters that look like they haven’t been invented yet, so set designers keep reaching for
the same few wide, geometric shapes.

@font-orbitron: DOCKING BAY 7 • AIRLOCK SEALED

**Orbitron**, by [Matt McInerney](designer:matt-mcinerney), is the one to reach for. Its designer’s own
explanation is that in every film about the future, all the other fonts seem to have been lost in the
apocalypse, and only a few geometric ones survived to go on spaceship hulls, station signs, and uniforms with
aerodynamic shoulder pads. It comes in four weights with plenty of alternates.

@font-zen-dots: HYPERDRIVE READY

**Zen Dots**, by Yoshimichi Ohira, was designed with a futuristic vision, drawing on science fiction films of
several eras. Its rounded, squared-off letters look like they belong on a control panel.

@font-silkscreen: ACCESS GRANTED

**Silkscreen** is a tiny pixel font [Jason Kottke](designer:jason-kottke) drew in 1999 for web graphics. It’s
since shown up at Flickr, Volvo, and Adobe, with Britney Spears and Carly Rae Jepsen, and on screen in *The
Bourne Legacy*: when a movie computer needs to look serious, a pixel font does the job.
