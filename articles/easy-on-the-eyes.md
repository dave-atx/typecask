These fonts were designed around readers who have a harder time with type: people with low vision or
dyslexia, or anyone with tired eyes at the end of a long day. Most of them work well for everyone, and some
are lovely in their own right.

A word on fonts for dyslexia: the research is mixed. Studies haven't found that they help most dyslexic
readers read faster or more accurately than a clear, ordinary font, though some people find them more
comfortable. Size, spacing, and line length often matter more than the shapes of the letters. If one feels
better to you, that's reason enough to use it.

## For low vision

@font-atkinson-hyperlegible: Il1 O0 rn m: no two letters look alike

**Atkinson Hyperlegible** comes from the [Braille Institute](foundry:braille-institute) and
is named after its founder, J. Robert Atkinson. It starts from a traditional [grotesque](term:grotesque) sans
serif and then breaks the rules wherever a letter could be mistaken for another, which sometimes means
unexpected shapes. [Atkinson Hyperlegible Next](font:font-atkinson-hyperlegible-next) adds more weights and
languages, and [Atkinson Hyperlegible Mono](font:font-atkinson-hyperlegible-mono) is a coding version.

@font-luciole: Comment dit-on « firefly » ? Luciole.
= How do you say "firefly"? Luciole.

**Luciole**, which is French for firefly, is new to the catalog. It took two years of work between France's
Regional Technical Center for Visual Impairment and the studio [typographies.fr](https://www.luciole-vision.com/),
following a dozen design criteria for readers with low vision, and it has become a standard for visual
impairment in France: tens of thousands of books have been printed in it.

## For reading comfort

@font-lexend: A little more room for every letter

**Lexend** was created by [Bonnie Shaver-Troup](designer:bonnie-shaver-troup) to reduce visual stress and improve
reading, at first with dyslexia and struggling readers in mind. [Thomas Jockin](designer:thomas-jockin) drew it in a range of widths
that get wider and more spaced out, named after metric prefixes: [Deca](font:font-lexend-deca),
[Exa](font:font-lexend-exa), [Giga](font:font-lexend-giga), [Mega](font:font-lexend-mega),
[Peta](font:font-lexend-peta), [Tera](font:font-lexend-tera), and [Zetta](font:font-lexend-zetta).

@font-opendyslexic: Heavy bottoms keep letters grounded

**OpenDyslexic**, by [Abbie Gonzalez](designer:abbie-gonzalez), gives every letter a heavier bottom, so
it's easier to tell which way is down and harder for letters like b and d to flip in your mind. It's one of
the best-known fonts made for dyslexia, and it's free.

## For code and writing

@font-intel-one-mono: for (i = 0; i < l; i++) { O0 = 1; }

**Intel One Mono** was designed by [Frere-Jones Type](foundry:frere-jones-type) with [Intel](foundry:intel) for developers
with low vision, to cut eye strain and coding errors. A panel of low-vision and legally blind developers
gave feedback at every stage of the design.

@font-ia-writer-quattro: Write first. Edit later. Format never.

**[iA Writer Mono](font:font-ia-writer-mono), [Duo](font:font-ia-writer-duo), and
[Quattro](font:font-ia-writer-quattro)** come from [iA](https://ia.net/), the makers of the iA Writer app,
and are built on [IBM Plex Mono](font:font-ibm-plex-mono) (see [Workhorses](article:workhorses)). Mono is
[monospaced](term:monospaced), like a typewriter; Duo gives a few wide letters like m and w double width; and
Quattro uses four widths. All three keep a typewriter's calm, even rhythm while reading more smoothly than a
true monospace.
