Most famous typefaces belong to someone: a foundry sells them, or they come with one company's computers.
The fonts here are free takes on them. Some are careful revivals of type cut centuries ago; others borrow an
idea and run with it.

Your iPhone or iPad already has a few of the originals, like Futura, Baskerville, and Bodoni 72. The free
fonts below earn their place with more weights, a different flavor, or a better fit on screen.

## Garamond, 1530s

[Claude Garamont](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Claude_Garamond) cut his roman type in Paris in the 16th
century, and type designers have been redrawing it ever since. Most fonts called
[Garamond](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Garamond) today are 20th-century reinterpretations, some closer to
the original than others.

@font-eb-garamond: Set from a specimen printed in 1592

**EB Garamond** goes back to the source: a type [specimen](term:specimen) printed in 1592 by Conrad Berner,
which showed Garamont's roman alongside Robert Granjon's italic. It follows that specimen
closely, so it reads more like an old printed book than most digital Garamonds. Use it for long reading at a
comfortable size.

## Baskerville, 1750s

[John Baskerville](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Baskerville), a printer in Birmingham, wanted sharper
type than anyone in England was printing, and made his own type, ink, and paper to get it. His typeface sits
between the [old style](term:old-style) of Garamond and the high [contrast](term:contrast) of Bodoni. iOS
already includes a Baskerville.

@font-libre-baskerville: Sharper type from a Birmingham printer

**Libre Baskerville**, from [Impallari Type](foundry:impallari-type), is based on American Type
Founders' 1941 version. It has a taller [x-height](term:x-height), wider [counters](term:counter), and a
little less contrast than the original, which helps it read well on a screen at body text sizes. Choose it
over the built-in Baskerville when the text is long and the type is small.

## Bodoni and the Didones, 1790s

[Giambattista Bodoni](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Giambattista_Bodoni) in Parma and the
[Didot family](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Didot_family) in Paris pushed contrast as far as it would go:
thick vertical strokes, hairline horizontals, and flat, unbracketed serifs. The look still says fashion
magazine. iOS includes Bodoni 72 and Didot.

@font-bodoni-moda: Hairlines, heavy stems and high fashion

**Bodoni Moda**, by [Owen Earl](designer:owen-earl) of [indestructible type](https://indestructibletype.com/), is a full Bodoni
family with every weight, italics, and optical sizes, so the hairlines stay delicate in a headline and
sturdy enough in smaller text.

@font-playfair-display: A Title Fit for the Spring Issue

**Playfair Display**, by [Claus Eggers Sørensen](designer:claus-eggers-sørensen), isn't a revival of any one typeface. It takes its cues from
the late 18th century, when pointed steel pens replaced broad quills, and mixes in Baskerville and Scotch
Roman. Set it big; it's made for titles.

## Franklin Gothic, 1902

Morris Fuller Benton drew [Franklin Gothic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Franklin_Gothic) for American Type
Founders, and it became a staple of American newspaper headlines and advertising. It's a sturdy,
plain-spoken sans serif with a two-storey g. "Gothic" was what American type founders called sans serifs
then (British founders said "[grotesque](term:grotesque)"), which is why so many American sans serifs of the
era carry the name. Just to keep things confusing, gothic can also mean [blackletter](term:blackletter), the
medieval style.

@font-libre-franklin: Headline News, Late City Edition

**Libre Franklin**, from Impallari Type, is an interpretation and expansion of Benton's design, with a full
range of weights for text as well as headlines.

@font-public-sans: Renew your permit online in five minutes

**Public Sans** is built on Libre Franklin by the [US Web Design System](https://designsystem.digital.gov/),
for government websites. It's a little more neutral, and made to work in interfaces as well as text and
headings.

## Alternate Gothic, 1903

Benton again: a year after Franklin Gothic, he drew a set of [condensed](term:condensed) gothics for
American Type Founders, made to squeeze a headline into a narrow newspaper column.

@font-league-gothic: LOCAL EDITOR FITS ENTIRE HEADLINE ON ONE LINE, READERS STUNNED

**League Gothic** is a revival of Alternate Gothic No. 1 by
[The League of Moveable Type](foundry:the-league-of-moveable-type), one of the first open-source type
foundries. It's tall and tightly packed, so a long title fits on one line of a slide or a poster.

## Futura, 1927

Paul Renner built [Futura](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Futura_%28typeface%29) from circles, triangles, and
squares, in the spirit of the Bauhaus. It has been on Volkswagen ads, Wes Anderson title cards, and the
plaque the Apollo 11 astronauts left on the Moon. iOS includes it.

@font-jost: Circles, triangles and squares, 1927

**Jost**, by Owen Earl, is inspired by the German [geometric](term:geometric) sans serifs of the 1920s rather
than copied from Futura. It's a [variable font](term:variable-font) that runs from a hairline thin to a heavy
black, with matching italics.

## DIN, 1931

The German standards body DIN published [DIN 1451](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/DIN_1451) in 1931, a
lettering standard for engineering drawings, signs, and the Autobahn. It was meant to be easy to reproduce
with simple tools, and it spread from road signs to anywhere that wanted to look engineered. iOS has two
cuts, DIN Alternate and DIN Condensed, both bold only.

@font-dinish: Ausfahrt 23 · Autobahn A9 · 120 km/h

**DINish** looks like DIN 1451 but was drawn with readers in mind, with small, deliberate departures from the
standard's grid-and-ruler rules. That makes it usable for body text, captions, and spreadsheets, not just
signs, and it comes in a range of weights and widths.

## Courier, 1955

Howard Kettler designed [Courier](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Courier_%28typeface%29) for [IBM](foundry:ibm)'s electric
typewriters, and it became the sound of a typewriter on paper. Screenplays are still set in it, since one
page of Courier works out to about a minute of screen time. iOS includes Courier New, a thinner version made
for computers.

@font-courier-prime: INT. COFFEE SHOP - NIGHT

**Courier Prime** was made for screenwriters by
[Quote-Unquote Apps](foundry:quote-unquote-apps). Its serifs are crisper, its counters a
little wider, its bold darker, and its italics more cursive. If you write scripts in an app that lets you
pick a font, this is the one.

## Helvetica, 1957

Max Miedinger and Eduard Hoffmann drew it at the Haas Type Foundry in Switzerland as Neue Haas Grotesk, and
it was renamed [Helvetica](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Helvetica), Latin for Swiss, to sell abroad. It
became the face of corporate logos, transit signs, and, until 2015, the iPhone itself. iOS still includes
it.

@font-nata-sans: Departures · Track 4 · Zürich HB

**Nata Sans**, by Daniel Uzquiano Cruz, is a grotesque sans serif made as an alternative to Helvetica. It's
a little wider and warmer, with a generous x-height for screens, and its variable [weight](term:weight) runs
from Thin to Black.

@font-fragment-mono: let swiss = "neutral, but make it code"

**Fragment Mono**, by [Wei Huang](designer:wei-huang), asks what Helvetica would look like as a coding font. It's built on Nimbus
Sans, URW's long-standing Helvetica clone, with every character redrawn to the same width.

## Comic Sans, 1994

Vincent Connare drew [Comic Sans](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Comic_Sans) at [Microsoft](foundry:microsoft) for the speech
balloons of a cartoon assistant, and it escaped onto every bake sale flyer in the world. Designers love to
hate it, but its loose, open letters are easy for many people to read. Apple offers Comic Sans MS itself as
a free download.

@font-comic-neue: Bake sale on Saturday! Bring cookies

**Comic Neue**, by [Craig Rozynski](designer:craig-rozynski), keeps the honest, casual feel of Comic Sans and beats its squashed, wonky
letters into shape. It's the version to use when you want friendly without looking like a mistake.

@font-comic-shanns-mono: print("hello, friend") # no judgment here

**Comic Shanns Mono** is [Jesus Gonzalez](designer:jesus-gonzalez)'s [monospaced](term:monospaced) take on Comic Shanns, [Shannon Miwa](designer:shannon-miwa)'s
Comic Sans–inspired font. Every letter is the same width, so it works in a terminal or a code editor.

## Berkeley Mono, 2022

Not every classic is old. [Berkeley Mono](https://usgraphics.com/products/berkeley-mono), a commercial
coding font by Neil Panchal at U.S. Graphics Company, won over a lot of programmers (including me) with its
compact, geometric, slightly retro-technical look.

@font-ioskeley-mono: const total = values.reduce((a, b) => a + b, 0)

**Ioskeley Mono** is [Ahmed Hatem](designer:ahmed-hatem)'s font in that spirit. He built it on
[Iosevka](https://typeof.net/Iosevka/), an open-source coding font that can be reshaped letter by letter, and
tuned it toward Berkeley Mono's compact, geometric feel. It comes in ten weights and two widths, and if you like
Berkeley Mono, Hatem suggests supporting the original too.
